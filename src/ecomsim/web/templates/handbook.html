Who you win, and how

Every customer joins a cohort that remembers how it was won, and the cohort churns at that channel's rate for the rest of the game. The channel is the paid channel you spend most on that month - {{ dl('3.1', 'Meta') }}, {{ dl('3.2', 'Google Search') }} or {{ dl('3.4', 'TikTok') }} - organic if you spend nothing, the marketplace for buyers won on {{ dl('4.1', 'Daraz') }}, and deal-driven if your discount ({{ dl('1.1', 'range prices') }} and {{ dl('2.2', 'site-wide discount') }}) is over 25%.

Keeping the customers you have

{{ dl('6.1', 'CRM and retention') }} reaches more of your customers the more you spend, at full effect from about PKR {{ '{:,}'.format(cust.crm_full) }} a month. At full effect it cuts churn by up to {{ pct(cust.crm_churn, 0) }} and lifts repeat orders by up to {{ pct(cust.crm_repeat, 0) }}. The first rupees work hardest: reach grows with the square root of spend.

CRM cannot reach customers who bought on the marketplace - they belong to Daraz, not to you. And an unhappy customer leaves whatever you spend: every star your rating sits below 4 raises next month's churn.

Lifetime value against acquisition cost

Lifetime value is what a customer is worth over {{ cust.horizon }} months: the orders they place, times your average order, times your margin before marketing. Acquisition cost is everything you spent on marketing divided by every new customer you won.

So the ratio rises three ways: customers who stay (channel, segment, CRM, rating), orders that earn more (price discipline, bundles, the free-delivery threshold), and marketing that wins customers for less. Active customers at the end are simply what you won minus what you lost - the same levers, added up.

The trade-off that runs through all four

Fast growth and loyal customers pull against each other. Discounts and cheap channels add customers quickly and lose them quickly; the channels and segments that stay are slower and dearer to win. Customer value is not about the most customers this month - it is about how many are still buying, and what they earn you, at the end.

Where teams usually lose these points: discounts over 25% that turn every new customer deal-driven; budgets tilted to TikTok for cheap clicks; CRM cut to fund acquisition; a customer service team that falls behind as orders grow; and a rating dragged down by the cheapest courier and supplier.

Refused and returned parcels

What parcels refused at the door (shipped both ways, paid for twice) and customer returns (shipped back, and about {{ pct(ops.write_off, 0) }} of what comes back cannot be resold) cost you, as a share of revenue.

Refused at the door. Only cash-on-delivery parcels are refused. About {{ pct(ops.refused[0].cod, 0) }} of orders are cash on delivery, and {{ pct(ops.refused[0].rto, 0) }} of those are refused. A {{ dl('9.2', 'discount for paying online') }} works twice: fewer customers choose cash on delivery, and fewer of those who still do refuse the parcel.

Returned after delivery. About {{ pct(ops.return_base, 0) }} of last month's deliveries come back, more or less depending on four things:

  • Your {{ dl('10.4', 'returns policy') }}: {% for label, m in ops.policies %}{{ label|lower }} ×{{ '%.2f'|format(m) }}{{ ', ' if not loop.last }}{% endfor %} the usual rate.

  • What you sell: some products come back more often than others - your report shows which.

Stock turns a year

How many times a year your stock sells through: cost of goods sold in a year over the stock you hold on average. In weeks of cover, {% for t, w in ops.weeks.items() %}{{ '%g'|format(t) }} turns is about {{ '%.1f'|format(w) }} weeks{{ ', ' if not loop.last else '.' }}{% endfor %}

Turns are set by how much you buy and how much you keep in reserve - {{ dl('7.1', 'stock to purchase') }} and {{ dl('7.5', 'safety stock cover') }} - and by your {{ dl('7.2', 'supplier') }}, whose lead time decides how much you must hold while an order is on its way and whose minimum order decides how much you must buy at once. Dropping lines that do not sell ({{ dl('1.3', 'range changes') }}) lifts them too, and so does selling more of the same stock.

A large minimum order makes a small business buy more than it sells in a month; a long lead time makes it hold more while it waits.

Service level

The orders you served out of the demand you had: what you lose when a product runs out. It moves with {{ dl('7.1', 'stock to purchase') }}, {{ dl('7.5', 'safety stock cover') }}, your {{ dl('7.2', "supplier's") }} lead time, sourcing (local stock lands in 9 days, imported in 22) and {{ dl('11.2', 'AI demand forecasting') }}.

Only a few points, but a stock-out also loses the sale, the customer and the rating that follow it.

The trade-off that runs through all four

Stock turns and service level pull against each other: hold too little and you run out, hold too much and your turns collapse and your cash sits on a shelf. Delivery success and refused parcels pull against cost: the reliable courier and the prepayment discount both cost money on every order. Operations is not about maximising any one number - it is about finding the point where the last rupee spent saves at least a rupee.

Where teams usually lose these points: a courier mix built on the cheapest option; no reason for customers to pay in advance; free returns on the products that come back most; marketing that promises more than the product; and eight or more weeks of stock bought to save a few percent - or so little that the best sellers run out.
