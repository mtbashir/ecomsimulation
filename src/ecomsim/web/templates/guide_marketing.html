Performance marketing, the way this game plays it

How to read what your campaigns did, how to aim a budget at the people who buy, and how to run a test that can actually tell you something. Read it once before campaign setup opens, then keep it beside the monthly report.

1 · Two decisions, not one

How much you spend and who it reaches are separate decisions, and the game keeps them apart.

The budget

Set on the decision form: Meta, TikTok and Google Search. It decides how much you spend and appears on your P&L. More budget buys more reach, with diminishing returns, and every team spending on the same channel pushes up its cost.

The aim

Set on the Campaigns page. It splits each budget into up to three campaigns, decides which products each one promotes, and to whom. It never changes what you spend — only how hard each rupee works.

Broad is the baseline. A channel with no campaigns runs one broad campaign: every product, everyone, everywhere. The report's vs broad column measures each campaign against exactly that. The best aim is worth roughly {{ '{:.0%}'.format(g_.cap) }} more visits per rupee, plus visitors who convert better; the worst costs about the same the other way. That is a real lever, but it cannot rescue a bad price, an empty warehouse or a product nobody wants.

3 · Reading them together

No single number diagnoses a campaign. Read them in pairs, and start at the bottom of the funnel: CVR and CAC before CTR.

What you seeWhat it usually meansWhat to try
Good CTR, poor CVRThe ad is interesting to people who do not buy the product. Classic wrong audience. Check age, gender and cities against who buys what this campaign promotes.
Poor CTR, fair CVRThe right people, not moved by what they see. Language and format; invest in creative (on the decision form).
CPM climbing month on monthToo many rivals on the channel, or an audience too small for its budget. Widen the audience, or move some budget to a less crowded channel.
Good ROAS, CAC above what a customer is worthEach order looks paid for, but the customer it brings in will not spend enough over the year to cover what it cost to find them. ROAS counts one order; LTV counts the customer. Aim at buyers who come back, and spend on retention, before spending more here.
One campaign strong, one weak, same channelProducts with different buyers are sharing one audience — or two audiences are chasing one product. Give each group of buyers its own campaign.
vs broad negativeThis campaign's settings reach fewer buyers than no targeting at all would. Read the findings under it. Reset to broad while you work it out; broad is never a mistake.

4 · Building an audience

Each product has a real buyer. Nobody will tell you who it is: the job is to reason it out, set a campaign on that reasoning, and let the numbers tell you whether you were right.

Five questions for every product

  1. Who pays for it? Not who uses it. The person holding the phone at checkout.
  2. How old, and which gender? Think of the actual buyer, not the category label. "Hygiene" and "haircare" each contain products bought by very different people.
  3. Where do they live? Premium habits concentrate in the big cities; everyday essentials travel everywhere.
  4. What do they follow? Interests are what someone watches and reads, not what they buy.
  5. How do they buy it? On impulse, after thinking, or because they ran out? That decides the channel and the objective.

The settings, and what each one does

  • Products Which lines this campaign advertises. Group products whose buyers look alike; separate the ones whose buyers do not.
  • Age · gender Nothing ticked means everyone. Each tick concentrates the ad on that group, and shrinks the audience.
  • Where {{ g_.tiers['T1'] }}; {{ g_.tiers['T2'] }} (Faisalabad, Multan, Hyderabad, Gujranwala, Peshawar, Sialkot); {{ g_.tiers['Rest'] }}.
  • Interests Up to three of: {{ g_.interests|join(', ') }}. Each one narrows the audience to people who follow it.
  • Language · format Urdu, English or Roman Urdu; feed posts, reels and stories, or carousel. Match how your buyer reads and scrolls.

5 · Channels and objectives

Meta

Facebook and Instagram. The largest audience and the most settings. Works for most products once the aim is right.

TikTok

The youngest audience and the cheapest attention. Suits products bought on impulse by young people; wastes money on household essentials.

Google Search

Only reaches people already searching. Excellent for things people run out of and look for; useless for things nobody thinks to search for.

ObjectiveWhat the platform optimises forUse it when
TrafficVisits. The baseline.You are not sure yet.
ConversionsPeople likely to order. Almost always, once you know who buys. Strongest for impulse products.
AwarenessReach: as many people as possible, as cheaply as possible. Rarely here. It buys eyeballs, not visits; brand-building spend on the decision form does that job better.
RetargetingPeople who visited your store recently. As a slice, never the whole budget. The pool of recent visitors is small: beyond about {{ '{:.0%}'.format(g_.retarget_share) }} of a channel's usual budget, the same people see the same ad until it stops working.

On Google, keywords decide whose searches you appear for: generic product terms, your own store's name (a small pool of people who already know you), or rivals' names (expensive, low intent). Exact match shows only for the search itself: fewer searches, closer intent — good for things people run out of, worse for things they browse.

6 · Too narrow, too broad

Every setting you add concentrates the ad on likelier buyers and shrinks the pool it can reach. Both effects are real.

Too broad

Most impressions reach people who will never buy. Nothing breaks; the money just works at the baseline. Findings stay quiet, vs broad sits near zero.

Too narrow

A small audience with a big budget sees the same ad again and again. CPM climbs, response falls, and the findings will say so. Either widen the audience or give that campaign a smaller share.

A campaign with two or three well-chosen settings usually beats one with every box ticked. Aim at the buyer; do not try to describe them to the last detail.

7 · A/B testing

Tick Run campaign 1 (A) against campaign 2 (B) under any channel. The test changes nothing about your business; it tells you which setup works harder, and how sure you can be.

Design it

  1. Change one thing. Same products, same everything, except the one setting you are testing. Change four and you learn that B is better, not why.
  2. Split the budget fairly. 50/50 gathers evidence fastest. A test on a sliver of spend almost never gathers enough orders to decide.
  3. Leave it alone. Results pool month after month while both sides stay unchanged. Edit either one and the count starts again.
  4. Test big ideas first. A large difference shows up in a month; a small one can take the rest of the year.

Read it

The winner is judged on orders per PKR 1,000, which captures both cheaper visits and better conversion. Confidence is how sure you can be that the gap is real and not luck.

  • 95% or more Call it. Move the budget to the winner, then test your next idea against it.
  • 80–95% Leaning. Keep both unchanged another month.
  • Below 80% Inside the noise. Either they really are about as good, or the test needs more orders.

Try an A/A test once: two identical campaigns. For a month or two one will look better — sometimes convincingly. That is what luck looks like, and it is why nobody should act on one month of a small test.

How many orders a test needs

Orders from both sides together, to spot a real difference at 95% confidence four times out of five. Compare it with the orders your channel makes in a month.

8 · A monthly routine

  1. Open the report. Paid media section: sort each channel's campaigns by CAC and vs broad. Read every finding.
  2. Check the test. If it has been called, act on it. If not, leave it alone.
  3. Fix one thing. The worst campaign, using the diagnosis table above. One change, so next month tells you whether it worked.
  4. Check the budget. A campaign working at +15% deserves more money; the decision form is where you give it.
  5. Write it down. Your board memo should say what you changed and what you expect to see. Next month, check whether you were right.
